The oven was instrumental to the annual banquet, but the power failed at four in the afternoon. Rosa opened the wardrobe beside the kitchen, found an old apron, and tied it around her waist. The patch on one pocket was compatible with a new stitch, though the repair looked hurried.

She had planned to chop vegetables for sixty guests. Her baby slept in a cradle near the balcony, and a troop of volunteers waited downstairs. What began as one cooking episode quickly became a cluster of problems.

A generalization would not help. Every stove behaved differently, and each recipe assumed a steady supply of electricity. The group had to create a new scope for the evening. They could prolong the meal, reduce the menu, or move outside.

A retired dentist proposed an outdoor kitchen using charcoal and gas. The fire would oxidize the metal grill, so they covered it with foil. They placed a new anode in the water heater and checked the old prescription for its safe use. The scientific details felt oddly intimate in a room filled with family.

A guard from the nearby fort arrived with news that a burglar had damaged a power line. The outage might last all night. Some volunteers began to waver. Others wanted to flee before the rain. Rosa asked them to foster one simple idea: make the next task possible.

The work became a lesson in energy. A compression pump moved water from a tank. A patrol checked the street for fallen cables. A cruise ship in the distance glowed with electric light, while the neighborhood depended on small batteries and careful circulation of food through cool rooms.

They had several options. One group could use the community center's generator. Another could cook cold dishes. A third could postpone the banquet. They chose a mixture, assigning each item to the method that used the least power.

Smoke rose from the grill. The scent was radiant and gorgeous, and the balcony offered shelter beneath its overhang. A pot of pickles and a basket of garlic sat on the table. Someone squeezed lemons; someone else stirred soup. The meal was moving again.

By eight, the hall was full. Guests brought candles and shared food. The blackout had not disappeared, but it no longer controlled the evening. People welcomed the chance to talk without screens.

At midnight, the lights returned. Nobody rushed home. The volunteers cleaned the kitchen and wrote down what had worked. Their notes were full of practical warnings: keep a manual can opener, label fuel, protect the generator from rain.

Rosa watched her daughter sleep beside the empty cradle. The banquet had survived because the community treated power as a shared resource and uncertainty as a problem to solve together.

The volunteers distributed the food evenly. A neighbor named Jesus offered a prayer, while another guest treated the moment as a postulate rather than a command. A dog began to wag its tail beneath the table. Darkness was a cloak around the room, but the candles made every face visible and the change in mood happened sharply.

One faulty circuit could retard the generator, and the noise seemed overwhelming. A tin of opium tablets from an old medical kit sat untouched in a cupboard. Nobody wanted to overwhelm the careful system with unnecessary risk. Rosa's uncle stepped outside to light a cigar. A rooster on a nearby roof lifted its head and made a comic silhouette. The family laughed, and the tension passed.

A cock crowed at sunrise, and the exhausted volunteers laughed again. They had cooked for a hundred people without wasting what mattered most: food, trust, and the willingness to share the work.

Before leaving, each volunteer wrote one lesson on a paper plate. Use less energy, waste less food, and never assume that a crisis will respect the schedule. The plates went on the wall as a practical map for the next gathering.
