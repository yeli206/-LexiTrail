The bus is late, so you open a video platform for a minute. A friend sends a cartoon, then a film report. You look up and twenty minutes have passed. The entertainment and amusement were free; the cost was harder to see.

The infinite feed feels new, but its basic ingredients are old. Before the Internet, an audience gathered in a theatre to watch a drama. A spectator paid for a seat in a studio or listened to a concert. A periodical arrived at home, carrying an editorial, a romance, or a short literary essay. A street pedlar sold songs and stories to people who could not afford a formal show.

Each method had a limit. A theatre had seats. A printed bulletin had limits, and even the most popular melody ended. The modern feed removes these limits. It can advertise beyond a retail shop and offer recreation at every hour. It can announce a tiny event to millions of people before the press has time to reflect.

That is why the word “feed” is too gentle. A feed suggests food arriving on a farm. A better framework may be a market. Every platform buys attention and then sells it to advertisers. Your time becomes inventory. A recommendation is not merely a friendly suggestion. It is a decision about what should come next.

The feed also changes memory. A journal or periodical once created a boundary: once the issue ended, the reader put it down. Online, one item always precedes another, and the next one may be even more interesting than the last. Novelty keeps people moving. A subtle pause can be enough time for another video to load.

Many users say they scroll only during a break, yet this pattern is now prevalent. The difference between a break and a habit has become blurred. A quick scroll is pleasant; private gossip can be amusing. But the pattern can slowly replace more nourishing forms of leisure. A slow ramble, a book, or a meal with friends may decline, not because they are boring, but because they require more effort at the beginning.

People still choose. Some form small groups around a common interest. Others subscribe to a newsletter and protect it as a daily ritual. A good creator builds trust rather than trying to win every second.

Danger also travels faster than before. A false claim can propagate through private chat, social posts, and news pages. The propagation may look organic even when it was planned. Groups can disperse into separate information worlds, each with its own heroes and villains. In this environment, popularity is not proof. A hit can simply mean that the content matched a powerful emotional pattern.

That does not make every popular thing worthless. Pop culture gives people shared language. A song can carry a private memory. A comedy can help a community laugh at pressure. A romantic story can make strangers feel less alone. Even a commercial product may connect people through a hobby. The problem begins when a company treats those emotions as raw material for endless consumption.

The old press was not innocent either. Newspapers chased scandal, and editors shaped what readers considered important. But the pace was slower. An editorial appeared after a deadline. A fact could be checked before the next edition. Today, speed is often given priority over accuracy. The first version spreads widely; the correction travels behind it. No formal declaration is needed for that race to begin.

What might a healthier framework look like? It would make time visible. It would let users set a stopping point before they open the app. It would give creators a clear route to support, without demanding constant novelty. It would not pretend that every free service is truly free.

The most useful question is not “How do I quit the Internet?” Most people cannot and do not want to. The better question is “What am I choosing instead?” If a ten-minute scroll replaces a moment of boredom, perhaps nothing is lost. If it replaces sleep, company, or work that matters, then the platform has made a decision for us.

Tonight, I put the phone in another room. The world becomes quiet, and no new bulletin arrives. For a while I walk around my neighborhood without recording it. Then a melody drifts from an open window. It is an old song, not a new hit. I stop for a moment. The moment is not content. It belongs to me.
