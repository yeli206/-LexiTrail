At dusk, a hymn drifted from the railway office. The old tune seemed to glorify the confidence of engineers who had built the line more than a century earlier. Ada listened for a moment, then turned back to the forecast. Tonight, confidence would not be enough.

The station stood fore of the harbour, therein linking the fishing quarter to the inland hills. Tourists liked to roam the track and take photographs of the sea. Freight trains carried food and fuel ashore. If the embankment failed, the town could be isolated for days.

A disastrous storm was moving north. A locomotive waited at the depot, and a dome-shaped water tank stood beside it. The tank was not the problem. The cylinder beneath the old pump was cracked, and compression inside the pipe was rising.

Ada had signed a subscription to a weather service. Its prediction was useful, but not prophecy. No scientist could foresee every local gust. A sailor who knew the coast offered another kind of forecast. He pointed to a red cloud and said the wind would turn before midnight.

The two methods were not rivals. Ada compared the official map with the sailor's intonation and local terms. A pattern shaped like a tulip spread across the screen. The overlap gave her enough confidence to act.

There was no villain and no plot to kidnap the town's attention. The danger was ordinary water moving with extraordinary force. Guilt would not help. Validity would: Was every gauge working? Was the evacuation route available? Were the materials strong enough?

A young marshal arrived with a truck of sandbags. He asked who should preside over the response. Ada named three people: one for the bridge, one for the tunnel, and one for the homes near the shore. The structure was clear, so no one had to wait for a hero.

Workers began to stalk the edge of the water, watching for cracks. They checked an axial joint where the rail crossed a culvert. The gauge was finite, but it still measured a rise. A magnet held a temporary sensor against the steel. Soon the reading became advantageous: it warned them before the eye could see the danger.

The storm hit harder than predicted. Waves battered the harbor wall, and the water tried to eject stones from the foundation. A roundabout below the station flooded. Workers moved a metal barrier and kept vehicles away.

For two hours, the embankment shook. Then the wind changed. The tide began to fall. The locomotive started safely and carried its cargo inland.

By morning, the track remained in place. Ada wrote a short report. The successful result came from hundreds of small decisions, not one dramatic idea. The seawall had held because people respected both the data and the place.

Before the storm, Ada had visited a mint on the overseas side of the line. Its walls carried the old brand of a railway company that had once shipped ore alongside passenger trains. She ducked beneath a stone dome and watched engineers undertake a test of the tunnel. Their instruments produced a vowel-like hum, and every measurement matched satisfactorily. In the nucleus of the control room, a young clerk used a preset alarm to warn them beforehand. Prevention, Ada knew, is rarely dramatic.

The sailor made no claim to be a prophet. He carried a metal bead in his pocket and called it a small excuse for luck. A shadowy cloud could hide a torpedo of wind just beyond the horizon. Ada did not question his experience. She asked what resources would avail them if the first barrier failed. The answer was ambitious: move every worker, protect the locomotive, and keep the availability of drinking water steady.

The vowel in the word held no special power, but every shouted instruction carried the same steady rhythm. By dawn, the crew understood that courage is often a matter of following a clear routine under pressure.
